# Situational Awareness

Make the state of the work easy to see, verify, and act on. A shared view should let a participant joining midstream answer what is happening, what changed, what is uncertain, who owns the next action, and where to check the evidence.

Use this skill with an existing team or a single agent and operator. When `steer-work` is available, it can manage runners and cadence. This skill covers what participants need to see and how they reason together. Use the access and authority already granted for the task.

## Build a shared view from the work itself

- Reuse the board, queue, manifest, map, or run record participants already consult. Link to authoritative artifacts and ledgers. Show the goal and current scope, latest verified changes, work in flight and its owner, holds and unresolved questions, and the next decisions or actions. Add timestamps, units, or versions where they affect a decision.
- Distinguish directly observed facts from attributed reports, hypotheses, and decisions. Give the observation time and evidence for consequential claims. Keep stages separate when they imply different next actions. An item acquired, submitted, returned, and independently reviewed can have four different statuses.
- Place state beside the object participants act on. In a spatial task, keep the selected area, neighboring coverage, the actual data footprint, and gaps visible together. In a digital workflow, show the current artifact version, owner, and dependencies together. Refresh volatile facts from their source before acting on them.
- Make consequential actions communicate naturally. A claim, hold, correction, or handoff should update the shared object or generate an event from the same action. Give affected participants access to relevant changes and keep linked detail available. Use a short checkpoint when automatic update is impractical. Refresh and read the shared view at meaningful boundaries and before acting on someone else's change.

## Distribute reasoning as well as tasks

Give existing participants distinct questions or kinds of judgment. One might examine evidence for a finding, another seek a competing explanation, and another check source quality or whether two findings share evidence. Keep action ownership clear while allowing peers to inspect the same evidence. Bound peer checks to decisions they can improve.

Keep corrections visible to those who depend on them. Record the original claim, corrective evidence, changed decision, and who should revisit affected work. Preserve unresolved disagreements and identify a check that could settle them. Trace apparent corroboration to its sources before counting it as independent. When a lesson recurs, carry it into future work through a retrieved example, a regression case, a tool check, or a revised procedure.

## Preserve communication when changing a workflow

Before automating or replacing an established channel, follow an ordinary case and an exception. Find who learns something incidentally, which cue they see, and what they do because of it. A faster direct handoff can remove the signal a neighboring worker needs to pause, correct a result, or lend help. Keep that signal visible where affected participants work.

Test the proposed workflow with a stale handoff, an unexpected hold, or a participant joining late. Check that the affected participant notices the change, can verify it, and knows what to do next. Review routine throughput alongside detection and recovery. Add structure where it changes those outcomes.

For a worked example of natural communication and the cues a redesign can remove, read [Norman’s cockpit example](references/cockpit.md).
