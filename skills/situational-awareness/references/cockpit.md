# The cockpit as a shared workspace

In chapter 6 of *Things That Make Us Smart*, Donald Norman uses a two-pilot cockpit to show how the arrangement of controls helps a crew stay aware of each other’s work. The captain and first officer can alternate who flies. Instruments in front of each pilot provide local views, and controls in the center are shared. The linked control wheels move together, so a pilot can see or feel the other pilot’s input.

## Natural communication

When the captain reaches across and lowers the landing-gear lever, the visible movement tells the first officer that the command was issued. The action also gives the captain a distinct memory of doing it. The same motion performs the work and communicates it. In an agent system, a tool that changes ownership or moves an item back to review can expose that change to collaborators as part of the operation. A shared view can show who acted, what was requested, and whether the resulting state has been verified.

## What a redesign can remove

In Norman’s example, independent side sticks remove the physical link between the pilots’ controls. The change removes one way each pilot could sense when the other was acting. A similar loss occurs when automation moves a visible handoff into a private message. The intended recipient gets the message, while someone monitoring a downstream step can miss the cue to pause.

Before replacing a shared workflow, watch an ordinary handoff and a handoff under pressure. Ask what the second participant sees that tells them who is acting, what has changed, and whether they should intervene. Then check that the new workflow exposes those cues at the point of work. In an agent workflow, reopen an item while a downstream worker is preparing to act. The worker should see the change and be able to verify current ownership and status before proceeding.

Source — Donald A. Norman, *Things That Make Us Smart: Defending Human Attributes in the Age of the Machine* (Addison-Wesley, 1993), chapter 6, pp. 139–143, especially Figure 6.1 and the landing-gear example on p. 142.
